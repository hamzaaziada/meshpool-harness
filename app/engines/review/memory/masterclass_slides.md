# The board-deck masterclass

## How we read your work

We read the cover and the titles first. Unless the brief requires a neutral cover, the cover should tell us the answer; the titles should then tell a complete story: the decision, why it is affordable, why it is preferable, and what happens next. We look at the executive summary to see whether the recommendation is sized and whether the real alternatives are visible.

Then we go straight to the number on which the recommendation depends. We trace it to its source, rebuild the calculation and check that the title says what the exhibit proves. On a funding decision, that usually means opening cash, every intervening flow, the trough and the minimum cash requirement. On an allocation decision, it means the alternatives, their common basis and the hurdle.

We stop when the bridge does not foot, the periods overlap, a key fact is stale or unsourced, or the conclusion claims more than the evidence supports. We also stop when the deck asks a board to approve something already declared, treats an authorization as cash spent, or proposes a threshold it cannot explain. A polished page does not compensate for any of these.

Our standard is not that every page contains everything. It is that every page has one job, does it visibly, and survives a skeptical reader without a voice-over.

## Storyline and titles

- **Put a sized answer first, unless the brief explicitly calls for a neutral cover.** State the action, amount and consequence on the cover or executive summary. *Wrong:* “Capital allocation review.” *Right:* “Fund $XB of investment and return $YB, leaving $ZB of cash.”

- **Make each content title a conclusion supported by that page.** Include the deciding number when it helps the reader understand the claim. *Wrong:* “Returns comparison.” *Right:* “Buying back at $X a share returns Y%, above the Z% cost of equity.”

- **Give each page a distinct question.** A decision page states the choice; a funding page tests capacity; an allocation page compares alternatives; a closing page assigns execution. *Wrong:* Repeat the cash argument on both evidence pages. *Right:* “Can we afford it?” followed by “Where should the next dollar go?”

- **Show the alternatives and their consequences before asking for a decision.** Use the same rows and baseline for every option, including doing nothing when it is a live choice. *Wrong:* Show only the recommended buyback. *Right:* Compare buyback, dividend and retention on cash used and cash remaining.

- **Separate recommendation from resolution.** The summary explains what we recommend and why; the closing states the motion, dependencies, owner and timing. *Wrong:* Two competing approval requests. *Right:* One recommendation up front and one executable resolution at the end.

- **Make the title-only read coherent.** Read the titles in order and remove a page that repeats an earlier conclusion without adding evidence. *Wrong:* Two titles both say cash is sufficient. *Right:* One states capacity; the next states the trade-off.

## The page: layout, exhibits and visual polish

- **Choose the exhibit for the question.** Use a waterfall for a closed cash bridge, a common-row table for options, and a line chart for a price-dependent hurdle. *Wrong:* A waterfall without an opening balance. *Right:* A dated sources-and-uses schedule from opening cash to trough.

- **Put the decisive test on the face of the exhibit.** Label the hurdle, floor, crossover or recommended row where the reader can see it. *Wrong:* Cash floor buried in a footnote. *Right:* Dashed floor on the bridge with the cushion labelled.

- **Keep one principal exhibit and one takeaway on an evidence page.** Move detailed derivations, secondary sensitivities and nonessential context to backup. *Wrong:* Funding waterfall, return chart and risk panel competing on one page. *Right:* Return chart and argument, with the EPS build in an appendix.

- **Limit the summary to decision-useful information.** Show the recommendation, the live options and the few numbers needed to size them; put supporting detail elsewhere. *Wrong:* A panel of debt, lease and interest figures obscures the decision. *Right:* An options table and a clear decision line.

- **Use distinct layouts when the messages differ.** Keep covers, summaries, bridges, comparisons and roadmaps visually recognizable; do not vary the design merely for variety. *Wrong:* The same dense table on every page. *Right:* A bridge for funding and a roadmap for execution.

- **Keep qualification next to the claim it limits.** A material caveat belongs on the page, not only in analyst notes or an appendix. *Wrong:* “Cash remaining $XB,” with omitted flows unstated. *Right:* “Illustrative residual $XB; excludes intervening flows.”

## Numbers and proof

- **Give every number its unit, period or as-of date, and source or formula.** Label ratios with their numerator and denominator; label changes as percentages or percentage points. *Wrong:* “Cover: X.” *Right:* “Cash at period-end / planned capex: Xx,” with both inputs sourced.

- **Rebuild every derived figure and propagate one answer.** Check bridges, per-share calculations, sensitivities and titles against the underlying inputs before checking the prose. *Wrong:* The summary and waterfall show different closing cash. *Right:* Both use the same reconciled opening balance, flows and closing balance.

- **Do not add past flows to a balance that already contains them.** A forward bridge starts at a dated balance and includes only subsequent flows; show trailing cash generation separately. *Wrong:* Add last year’s free cash flow to year-end cash. *Right:* Use a sourced forward estimate or label a flat-flow assumption.

- **Distinguish actual, guidance, consensus, assumption and hypothetical.** Say when a balance is historical rather than current, and label illustrative cases as cases. *Wrong:* Present last year’s operating cash flow as next year’s forecast. *Right:* “Next-period cash flow assumed flat to the reported year.”

- **Compare alternatives on a genuinely common basis.** State amount, period, tax treatment and what each measure represents. Do not call earnings yield a cash return or compare enterprise and equity measures as if they were identical. *Wrong:* Rank pretax cash yield against after-tax company return without qualification. *Right:* Align the basis where possible and label remaining differences.

- **Show the full economics of a buyback.** State the share price and share count, calculate shares retired and gross EPS effect, then deduct the funding cost to show net EPS effect. *Wrong:* “EPS rises $X/share” before interest forgone. *Right:* “Gross uplift $X/share, less after-tax interest forgone $Y/share, equals net effect.”

- **Do not turn a historical average into a marginal project return.** Source incremental economics or make the recommendation conditional on obtaining them. *Wrong:* “The next $XB earns X%,” based on company-wide historical return. *Right:* “Existing capital earned X%; the return on the proposed projects is not yet established.”

- **Show the downside and the decision threshold when they matter.** Recalculate the cash balance, cover and cushion in each supported case; derive a crossover price or drop it. *Wrong:* “Pause above $X” with no basis. *Right:* “Pause at the price implied by EPS divided by the stated hurdle,” with inputs shown.

## Banker and consulting conventions

- **Use primary evidence for decision-critical facts.** Cite the filing or company release, relevant date and location; preserve qualifiers such as “approximately” and “up to.” *Wrong:* An aggregator is the sole source for capex guidance. *Right:* The primary disclosure is cited and its scope is retained.

- **Describe the legal and decision status of each item.** Separate declared, proposed, authorized, drawn and paid amounts. *Wrong:* Ask the board to declare an already-declared dividend. *Right:* Confirm its terms and funding, then identify the decisions still open.

- **Treat an authorization as a ceiling, not an outflow.** Show remaining capacity, expiry, proposed pace and actual assumed draw separately. *Wrong:* Count the full authorization as cash returned today. *Right:* “Up to $XB authorized; $YB assumed drawn in the period.”

- **Label policy choices and estimates honestly.** A cash floor, valuation threshold or tax assumption needs a source, derivation or explicit policy label. *Wrong:* “Management minimum cash: $XB,” without evidence. *Right:* “Proposed board cash buffer: $XB,” with its basis.

- **Close with a motion and a control system.** Give every action an owner, date or cadence, dependency, and—where material—a measurable pause or escalation trigger. *Wrong:* “Monitor liquidity.” *Right:* “Treasury updates the cash forecast before the next board meeting; pause draws below the approved buffer.”

## Language

- **Write for the board, not for the analyst.** Remove instructions to research, placeholders and production notes from the finished page; retain genuine open questions as decision gates. *Wrong:* “Find the missing project return.” *Right:* “Release capital only after the CFO confirms project returns.”

- **Use the narrowest claim the evidence supports.** Distinguish preserving optionality from proving superiority, and a scenario from a forecast. *Wrong:* “Growth will earn X%.” *Right:* “Historical company return was X%; incremental project returns require confirmation.”

- **Name discrepancies rather than smoothing them away.** State which input is used, why, and what remains unresolved. *Wrong:* Silently switch dividend run-rates across pages. *Right:* “Reported rates differ; use the declared rate pending reconciliation.”

- **Use direct verbs and defined terms.** Say “approve,” “fund,” “pause” or “confirm,” and specify what each applies to. *Wrong:* “Optimize capital deployment.” *Right:* “Pause repurchases until the liquidity forecast is confirmed.”

## The mistakes you keep making

In rough order of recurrence, these are the rejections we see most often:

1. **The cash walk does not foot.** Rebuild it from dated inputs, include every relevant use, and carry the result into the title and summary.
2. **You mix periods or double-count flows.** Separate historical actuals from forward assumptions; never add an old flow to a balance that already includes it.
3. **The same number changes across pages.** Maintain one calculation and reconcile every title, table, trigger and closing motion to it.
4. **Historical company return becomes a project forecast.** Label it as historical and company-wide; source marginal economics or make the investment decision conditional.
5. **You confuse an authorization with a payment.** Show the ceiling, remaining capacity, pace and assumed draw separately.
6. **You mishandle a declared transaction.** Establish what has already been decided and frame the board’s remaining choices around it.
7. **The comparison mixes bases.** Align amount, time, tax and denominator; explain measures that cannot be made equivalent.
8. **A decisive fact is stale, secondary or unsourced.** Refresh it from the primary source, date it and recompute every dependent figure.
9. **The recommendation has no defensible sizing rule.** Show the cash buffer, investment need and opportunity cost—or label the size as judgment.
10. **The summary and evidence pages repeat one another.** Give each page one question; move detailed proof to the page that owns it.
11. **A threshold is asserted rather than derived.** Calculate the hurdle or price crossover, label it as policy, or remove it.
12. **Gross buyback accretion is presented as the whole case.** Include forgone after-tax interest and show the net effect.
13. **The downside is missing or does not recalculate.** Use supported scenarios and recompute the trough, cover and cushion for each.
14. **The close is a summary, not an action.** Write the resolution, owners, timing, dependencies and change conditions.

## What an 8 looks like

We would sign off a concise, answer-first deck in which the recommendation is sized and the actual board decision is unmistakable. The summary shows the real choices on a common basis. A dated cash bridge ties from a sourced opening balance to the relevant trough, with the cash floor and exclusions visible. One exhibit makes the allocation argument against a labelled hurdle; its calculations are reproducible and its limitations are on the page.

The supporting model has traceable inputs, consistent periods, formulas that reconcile, and a downside that changes the outputs rather than merely changing the labels. The close gives the board a resolution and management a dated sequence of actions, with owners and triggers. Every title can stand alone; every important number can be checked.

## Before you send it

Answer **yes** to each question:

- [ ] Does the cover or executive summary state the sized recommendation, consistent with the brief?
- [ ] Do the titles alone tell a coherent, nonrepetitive story?
- [ ] Does each page answer one distinct decision question?
- [ ] Are the live alternatives shown on the same baseline?
- [ ] Is each material number labelled with its unit and relevant period or date?
- [ ] Can each material input be traced to a source, and each derived figure to a formula?
- [ ] Have the decision-critical facts been checked against current primary sources?
- [ ] Does every bridge foot, including all material cash uses?
- [ ] Are historical flows kept separate from the forward cash walk?
- [ ] Are actuals, guidance, assumptions and hypothetical cases clearly distinguished?
- [ ] Do the summary, exhibits and closing use the same amounts, thresholds and definitions?
- [ ] Are declared payments, proposed actions and optional authorizations clearly separated?
- [ ] Are comparisons aligned on time, tax, denominator and economic meaning?
- [ ] Are gross and net effects both shown where funding cost matters?
- [ ] Are the downside, floor and any crossover calculated and labelled?
- [ ] Is any missing evidence visible, with the recommendation made conditional where necessary?
- [ ] Does the closing specify the motion, owners, timing, dependencies and triggers?
